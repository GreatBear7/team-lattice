Using the tool

ResponseWatch is for an on-duty 911 dispatch supervisor reviewing a batch of completed high-priority ambulance calls. It answers three questions: which district, which time of day, and which response stage needs investigation.

1. Choose data. Pick Normal operations, Recovery or Morning tail at the top of the dashboard, or open API data and upload your own CSV (same columns as the data below).

2. Scan the six districts. Late is the share of calls over 12 minutes; amber means it is above the 10% target. P90 is the time 90% of calls arrive within. Change says whether the district got worse than its own normal: red “daily” counts flagged days, red “bands” counts flagged times of day.

3. Open a district. Daily p chart shows which days were worse than normal. Where & when shows which hours: the heatmap and 24-hour clock are descriptive, Band comparison is the statistical test. Delay stages shows whether call handling, turnout or travel slowed down.

4. Check the slowest calls. Severe individual delays lists the five longest responses, so a few very long waits stay visible even when no rate is flagged.

FAQ

What does a red triangle on the daily p chart mean?

That day's late rate was above the district's upper control limit: more late calls than ordinary day-to-day variation explains. It is a signal to investigate, not proof of a cause.

A district is amber (“above target”) but has no flag. Is that a contradiction?

No. They answer different questions. Above target compares the district with the city's goal (at most 10% late). Flagged compares it with its own past. Outer districts E and F miss the target every day because they are far from stations, but they are not getting worse, so they are not flagged.

Why does Morning tail show no flagged days but a flagged band?

District D's delays happen only between 06:00 and 10:00. The other 20 hours of each day are normal, so each day's overall rate stays under its upper limit (worst day 21.0% vs limit 24.6%). Pooling just the morning calls over 14 days gives enough calls to see the change: 37.1% late vs 17.9% before, z = 5.05. See the band test.

What do the grey hatched cells mean?

Too little data to judge: the baseline for that cell has fewer than 5 late or fewer than 5 on-time calls. The tool shows “insufficient evidence” instead of an all-clear; it never treats missing evidence as normal.

Why is the baseline frozen?

If the baseline were recalculated with the bad days included, a long problem would slowly redefine “normal” and hide itself. The first 14 days set each district's normal once, and every later day is compared against that.

Can I use my own data?

Yes. Open API data on the dashboard and upload a CSV with at least incident_id, zone_id and the four timestamps (with a timezone offset). The tool validates every row and lists errors instead of guessing. Limit: 25 MB. It needs 14 days of baseline before it can draw control limits.

Does a flag mean someone did something wrong?

No. A flag says the numbers changed by more than chance explains. The cause could be traffic, call volume, a closed station or anything else. The tool never ranks dispatchers or crews.

Is this live data?

No. It reviews completed calls after the fact, and every incident in the three test datasets is synthetic, generated by data/generate_data.py with a fixed seed.

Glossary

Response time R, minutes
On scene minus crew notified (④ − ②).
Late T = 12 min
A call is late when R > 12 minutes. T is a demo setting, not a clinical standard.
Late rate p
Late calls ÷ all calls, for a day, a band or a whole period.
Service target q = 10%
The city's goal: at most 10% of calls late. A policy choice, not a statistic. Amber = above target.
Baseline
The first 14 days (Sep 14–27). Sets each district's normal, once.
Monitoring
The days judged against the baseline (Sep 28 – Oct 11).
Normal late rate p₀, centre line
A district's late rate over its whole baseline. The teal line on the p chart.
Control limits UCL, LCL, 3σ
The range a day's late rate normally stays in: p₀ ± 3 standard errors. Orange dashed lines.
Upper-limit signal flag, red triangle
A monitoring day above the upper control limit: worse than the district's normal.
P90 “90% arrive within”
The response time 90% of calls beat. Shows the slow tail that averages hide.
Time band
Five parts of the day: night 00–06, morning rush 06–10, midday 10–16, evening rush 16–20, late evening 20–24 (local time).
Band test z
Compares a band's monitoring late rate with the same band's baseline. Flag when z > 3.
Exploratory
6 districts × 5 bands = 30 tests, so an occasional chance flag is possible. Treat band flags as leads.
Change (pp)
Percentage points: monitoring late rate minus baseline late rate, ×100. Used by the heatmap and clock.
Insufficient evidence
Too few calls to judge. Shown as grey hatch or a label; never treated as normal.
Delay stages
Call handling (① → ②), turnout (② → ③) and travel (③ → ④).
Over T
In the slowest-calls list: how many minutes past the 12-minute threshold.

Statistics: formulas and calculations

Every number on the dashboard comes from one tested file, app/qc.py. Worked examples below are its actual output.

1. Daily p chart (one per district)

Each day's calls are a pass/fail sample (late or not), so the late count is binomial. A p chart is the standard control chart for a proportion.

pi = latei / ni     p₀ = baseline late / baseline calls
UCL = min(1, p₀ + 3·√(p₀(1 − p₀)/ni))     LCL = max(0, p₀ − 3·√(p₀(1 − p₀)/ni))

A monitoring day is flagged when pi > UCL (exactly equal does not flag). Below the LCL means unusually few late calls, which is good news, so it is not flagged.

Worked example: Recovery, district C.

Baseline: 184 late of 1,400 calls → p₀ = 13.14%. Each day has n = 100.

3·√(0.1314 × 0.8686 / 100) = 3 × 0.0338 = 0.1014

UCL = 13.14% + 10.14% = 23.28%; LCL = 13.14% − 10.14% = 3.01%. Oct 1–6 are above 23.28% (up to 40%), so those six days are flagged; Oct 7–11 are back inside.

Limits for n = 100 calls a day. The baseline is identical in all three test datasets, so these limits are too.

Assumptions. Calls are roughly independent and the baseline is stable and representative. The tool checks n·p₀ ≥ 5 and n·(1 − p₀) ≥ 5 before drawing limits, and needs 14 baseline days with at least 5 late and 5 on-time calls. Real late calls can cluster (storms, busy hours), which would widen the true spread; a Laney p′ chart is the standard remedy and is not implemented.

2. Band test (pooled two-proportion z test)

For each district and time band, pool all monitoring days and compare with the same band's baseline. Both samples are a few hundred calls, so the test accounts for sampling error in both, not just the monitoring side.

p̂ = (latebase + latemon) / (nbase + nmon)
z = (pmon − pbase) / √( p̂(1 − p̂) · (1/nmon + 1/nbase) )

Flag when z > 3 (one-sided: only “worse” matters). The band's limit line is the monitoring rate at which z would equal exactly 3 for these sample sizes.

Worked example: Morning tail, district D, 06:00–10:00.

Baseline 50 late of 280 = 17.86%. Monitoring 98 late of 264 = 37.12%.

p̂ = 148 / 544 = 0.2721    SE = √(0.2721 × 0.7279 × (1/264 + 1/280)) = 0.0382
z = (0.3712 − 0.1786) / 0.0382 = 5.05 > 3 → flagged

The equivalent limit is 28.71%. District D's other four bands have negative z.

Why not the p chart formula? Treating the band baseline as exact gave at least one false alarm in 57% of 1,000 simulated normal months. The pooled test brings that to 3.7%, close to the roughly 4% expected from 30 comparisons at z > 3.

3. Hourly heatmap and 24-hour clock (descriptive)

change (pp) = 100 × (pmon, hour − pbase, hour)

144 cells (6 districts × 24 hours), each with only about 20–130 calls, so cells are coloured but never tested. The 3h option pools each hour with its neighbours (23:00 wraps to 00:00). Black outlines come from the band test above.

Example: Morning tail, district D, 08:00–09:00. Baseline 16.5% of 85 calls, monitoring 39.0% of 77 calls → +22.5 pp.

4. P90 (“90% arrive within”)

sort R; h = (n − 1) × 0.9; P90 = v[⌊h⌋] + (h − ⌊h⌋) × (v[⌊h⌋+1] − v[⌊h⌋])

Linear interpolation between the two nearest sorted values. Example: Recovery district C has P90 = 17.6 min over the 14 monitoring days, so 1 call in 10 waited longer than 17.6 minutes. With T = 12 and q = 10%, P90 above 12 minutes goes with a late rate above 10%: P90 shows how far past target a district is. P90 is descriptive; it has no control limits.

5. Delay stages

call handling = ② − ①    turnout = ③ − ②    travel = ④ − ③    (minutes)

Median and P90 of each stage, baseline vs monitoring. A stage whose P90 grows while its median barely moves points to a few very slow calls.

6. How we know the numbers are right

Known answers. p₀ = 0.10, n = 100 gives UCL 0.19 and LCL 0.01; 20 late calls flags, 19 does not. Band test: 120/600 vs 180/600 gives p̂ = 0.25, SE = 0.025, z = 4.0 (flag); a case with z exactly 3 does not flag.

Edge cases. No calls, all late, none late, too-short baseline, hour and band boundaries, and daylight-saving shifts all return an explicit status instead of a false all-clear.

Planted problems. Normal: no flags. Recovery: district C flagged Oct 1–6 plus two bands. Morning tail: district D morning band only. More than 200 automated tests check the statistics and the API.

Reference: D. C. Montgomery, Introduction to Statistical Quality Control, ch. 7 (control charts for attributes); the two-sample test for proportions in any applied statistics text.
